Chicago Communities Untouched by a Century of Redistricting

Amidst attacks on the Voting Rights Act, redistricting history may offer a new way to think about racial inequity in ward maps.

When the city council redraws ward maps every decade, it shapes who gets a seat at the table when wards decide where money, services, and attention go. The ward map is a statement about which communities deserve to be kept entirely in one ward, and which ones get split across four, five, or sometimes even six wards. Since the Voting Rights Act of 1965, redistricting has been an important tool for helping Black, Latino, and Asian voters elect representatives of their choice, rather than being cracked apart or packed into a single ward so their political power disappears. The Supreme Court, in Louisiana v. Callais 2026, severely weakened this tool by limiting how much mapmakers may consider racial demographics when drawing district lines. As a result, communities of color will have fewer ways to contest maps that weaken their representation.

This raises an urgent question: if racial demographics become harder to use as a basis for fair maps, what else could? Our research suggests exploring one answer: redistricting history. Some communities have been shielded from redistricting for a century while others have been carved up again and again. Those histories are public record, and they reveal patterns of political inequity grounded in the material past. This site, based on our study published in Geoforum, invites visitors to explore redistricting history as a new basis to think about map fairness. Data for all of Chicago's ward maps, along with our analysis, can be downloaded here.

Our historical approach

Our research question is simple: Which parts of Chicago have never changed wards? To answer this, we scanned and digitized all of Chicago's ward maps from the city's founding in the 19th century to the present. Over the course of its history, Chicago has drawn and redrawn its ward maps 21 times, in the following years:

1837 · 1847 · 1857 · 1863 · 1869 · 1876 · 1890 · 1900 · 1910 · 1912 · 1923 · 1931 · 1947 · 1961 · 1970 · 1981 · 1985 · 1995 · 2005 · 2015 · 2023

We also created a hexagonal grid over the city (with each hexagon roughly the square mileage of a city block) and assigned each hexagon's centroid to the ward in which it resided. We assigned wards to hexagons instead of city blocks because, over the course of Chicago history, the size of city blocks change. Hexagons provide a stable, though not perfect, way to measure spaces consistently over time.

This allowed us to identify what we call "untouched communities," or the small areas of Chicago that have never changed wards. Once you can see them, the idea of fair maps looks different.

Why this matters

Redrawing ward boundaries has real consequences, with studies showing it can affect crime rates, 311 service requests, and resource allocation across communities. When a neighborhood is shuffled into a new ward, the connections between residents, their alderperson, and city services are disrupted, and the people living there can pay the price. The reverse is just as important: stability accrues advantages. Political scientists have shown that more representation shapes the flow of public dollars, with politically over-represented areas receiving larger shares of public funds. Communities whose political footing never gets disturbed tend to accumulate resources over time, while communities that are constantly redrawn keep starting over. Neighborhoods untouched by redistricting are exactly the ones best positioned to hold onto political power and public resources, while the neighborhoods that get consistently redrawn absorb the disruption.

What we found

Roughly 15% of Chicago's land mass has never changed wards since the city moved to a system of 50 wards since 1923. These untouched areas are not located in random places. They cluster in 28 pockets, and 20 of them sit inside former independent settlements that were annexed into Chicago in the 1800s: places like Hyde Park, Beverly, Edison Park, Rogers Park, Norwood Park, and Lake View. The history of Chicago's formation is essential to understanding why.

Neighborhoods like Edison Park and Hyde Park were self-governing towns and villages, many of them developed for and by White professionals, merchants, and investors who bought in early and built institutions to protect their property. Chicago grew by annexing these settlements, and state law (e.g. the Annexation Act of 1889) ensured that annexed settlements like Edison Park and Hyde Park received ward representation in the city council. Over the course of Chicago's history, state laws and court-ordered ward remaps quietly protected Chicago's annexed settlements by referring to them as "natural" areas of Chicago that ward remaps should not split up.

The result has been a pattern that has held for over a century: the state's political protection of more affluent founding settlements. As a result of this history, combined with changes in redistricting case law, the burden to creatively draw new ward boundaries fell on Black, Latino, immigrant, and working-class communities. Even court-ordered "minority-majority" wards had to be carved out of existing Black and Latino regions of the city. This process transformed ward redistricting into a battle of race and class politics within White, Black, and Latino communities, all while the core areas of the city's founding settlements stayed largely untouched.

How protection worked

Untouched communities were shielded again and again through a chain of legal and political decisions, a process we call codification, where each generation wrote the previous one's advantages into a new kind of rule. It unfolded in three eras.

1. The settlements were founded between the 1830s and 1880s. Before there was a ward map to protect, there were the settlements themselves. After the U.S. took the land from indigenous peoples through the 1821 and 1833 treaties of Chicago, investors bought parcels on the city's edge and incorporated their own towns as residential quarters for white professionals. Paul Cornell founded Hyde Park; Conrad Sulzer, Lake View; Philip Rogers, Rogers Park; Christian Ebinger, Edison Park. These towns built early institutions to defend their property, and this became the foundation for everything to come.

2. Protection was written into state law from 1889–1931. As Chicago grew by absorbing these towns, their residents worried that annexation meant paying the city's taxes without getting its services in return, or being carved up and outvoted. Illinois answered with the Annexation Act of 1889, which guaranteed that a qualifying settlement, once annexed, "shall constitute a ward" of the city. In one stroke, the state turned a set of private real-estate ventures into permanent political units. Hyde Park, Lake View, Rogers Park and the rest entered Chicago intact, with their core communities represented in city council, and with a legal shield against being split.

3. The courts kept it in place (1931–2015). State-law protection eventually gave way to a new arena: the courtroom. After the Supreme Court's Reynolds v. Sims (1964) forced governments to redraw districts with equal population, Chicago's ward maps repeatedly ended up in litigation, and judges became the mapmakers' referees. Time and again, they described the founding settlements as "natural areas" of the city that should stay whole. The consequence fell elsewhere. Even when courts found a map discriminatory, such as in Ketchum (1985) and Barnett (1997), they ordered the new majority-Black and majority-Latino wards carved out of existing Black and Latino subareas of the city, and never out of the founding settlements.

Three different eras produced the same result across Chicago history. As Chicago enters a post-Voting Rights Act era of ward redistricting, our findings suggest that a new era of codification is approaching that will once again create a basis for legitimizing the city's entrenched neighborhood political inequalities. We hope that sharing this history will provide Chicagoans with a more complete understanding of ward history to inform the drawing of the first post-Louisiana v. Callais 2026 ward map.

About

The UChicago Justice Project

The UChicago Justice Project (UCJP) produces rigorous, independent research on policing, technology, and political inequality in Chicago and beyond. We pair advanced data and geographic analysis with archival and historical research to make visible the structures that shape who gets represented, protected, and served in American cities. We release our data, code, and findings publicly so that residents, journalists, advocates, and policymakers can use them. This project is part of UCJP's ongoing work to bring transparency and evidence to the systems that govern city life.

Methodology

To test whether the untouched areas were systematically linked to Chicago's former independent settlements, we estimated logistic regression models at the census-tract level for publicly available data from 1965-onward. Unfortunately, census tract boundaries are not standardized for all years prior to 1965 to enable a full analysis across all of Chicago history. Our approach enabled us to control for population size, racial and ethnic composition, income, education, and the foreign-born population. We analyzed whether tracts located on former settlements were more or less likely to remain in the same ward. Results showed they were significantly more likely to stay put, indicating that the pattern is not explained by ordinary demographic differences alone.

Our study also made use of qualitative data. We conducted archival research to understand how these communities avoided redistricting. Drawing on historical ward maps, newspaper coverage, records from redistricting lawsuits and city council deliberations, land and property records, and archival collections, we traced three recurring mechanisms: the incorporation of independent settlements into the city, the codification of their boundaries through law and precedent, and the adjudication of redistricting disputes in ways that repeatedly treated these founding areas as "natural" communities not to be divided.

Together, these methods help us paint a picture of some of the forces contributing to untouched communities. We hope that users make use of our data to build upon and advance our understanding of Chicago's ward redistricting histories.
